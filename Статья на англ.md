# The Token Worked. The Heating Didn’t.

![DIGITAL CITIES? — by YuRich](article-assets/digital-cities/cover.png)

*Conceptual cover illustration, AI-generated. Not a photograph of a real project.*

*I researched 45+ digital-city projects. The most important lesson wasn’t about blockchain. It was about who answers when the real world stops working.*

In late October 2025, a house in Detroit reportedly lost its heating. Months later, the problem appeared in a city court motion.

The tokens linked to the property companies still existed. The blockchain records remained intact.

By July 2026, a RealT co-founder was telling investors that the business was entering voluntary liquidation. [1]

**The token worked. The home didn’t.**

That gap is where my research into digital cities and tokenized real estate became much more interesting.

Because a city is not a collection of assets that happen to have tokens.

It is a collection of obligations that somebody must keep fulfilling.

Heating. Repairs. Taxes. Bookings. Payments. Disputes.

And, occasionally, an elevator that needs fixing before the DAO finishes voting.

## Beautiful cities. Uncomfortable questions.

In Italo Calvino’s *Invisible Cities*, Marco Polo describes extraordinary cities to Kublai Khan. Reading it, I kept thinking about our industry: the description can become so compelling that we forget to ask what exists outside it.

In crypto, that description comes with a ticker.

Sometimes a citizenship application. Sometimes an AI mayor. Sometimes an investment opportunity that promises to turn an entire city into an asset.

For my research, I mapped more than 45 projects across several categories: state-backed developments, private cities, local payment networks, urban AI and tokenized assets.

I stopped treating them as one market.

A city-branded coin, a membership pass, a property investment and a mobility-testing campus are different products. Putting “ecosystem” above all four does not make their economics interchangeable.

I now start with four questions:

**Who owns the asset and controls the money?**

Not “the community.” I want an entity, a title, an account and identifiable decision-makers.

**What does the buyer receive?**

Access? Equity? A payment claim? A collectible? Those answers lead to very different outcomes.

**Where does the money come from—twice?**

Once to launch the project. Again to keep operating and meet its obligations.

**What survives when someone changes their mind?**

A sponsor leaves. A government changes policy. A partner stops accepting the membership. What can the holder actually enforce?

Here is what happened when I applied those questions to real cases.

## 1. RealT: the risk was outside the contract

![Detroit housing in Outlier Media’s reporting on RealT. This is not identified here as the property in the heating complaint.](article-assets/digital-cities/realt-detroit.jpg)

*Detroit housing in Outlier Media’s reporting on RealT. This is not identified here as the property in the heating complaint. Source: [Outlier Media](https://outliermedia.org/crypto-real-estate-realt-cryptocurrency-detroit/).*

RealT was one of the most recognizable examples of tokenized rental property: small investment tickets, property-linked tokens and rental distributions.

That made it an important test—not just of tokenization, but of the operating structure beneath it.

The chronology below follows Coinpaprika’s October 12, 2026 review, which draws on court documents and reporting by Detroit’s Outlier Media. I am attributing that chronology rather than presenting it as my own inspection of every court filing. [1]

**July 2, 2025.** Detroit sued RealT’s founders and 165 affiliated companies over more than 400 properties lacking certificates of compliance.

**July 22, 2025.** A judge barred rent collection and directed tenants’ payments into an escrow restricted to repairs.

**January 2026.** A city motion stated that a house at 3510 Ewald Circle had lacked heating since late October 2025.

**February 2026.** RealT told investors it was suspending weekly distributions.

**April 2026.** A court-approved special fiduciary took control of roughly 700 Detroit properties.

**July 2, 2026.** A co-founder told investors the business was entering voluntary liquidation and that assets would be sold. [1]

One distinction matters here: **an announced voluntary liquidation is not the same thing as a filed bankruptcy proceeding.** Coinpaprika reported that its October 5 search found no bankruptcy petition by RealT entities. [1]

Another distinction matters even more.

Some coverage highlighted approximately **$640,000 in escrow**, against reports of around **$140 million raised** and **14,000–22,000 investors**. [2]

Dividing that balance by the number of investors produces a dramatic headline.

It does **not** produce a valid recovery estimate.

According to the more detailed account, the city’s rent escrow was restricted to repairs—not a pool waiting to be distributed to token holders. Recovery would depend on the relevant entities, property-sale proceeds, liabilities, fees and holders’ contractual position. [1]

I would rather lose a punchy statistic than build an argument on the wrong denominator.

The deeper lesson is uncomfortable enough without it.

RealT’s structures gave holders interests in property companies. Those interests were not interchangeable with direct, unencumbered ownership of a house. Under the offering terms reviewed by Coinpaprika, debts and fees came before distributions to holders in a liquidation. [1]

And the failures described in that review were not a smart-contract exploit. They involved property maintenance, taxes, titles, reserves and oversight. [1]

**A token can record an interest in a company. It cannot make that company a competent landlord.**

There is also concentration risk. Hundreds of properties exposed to the same local enforcement environment do not become geographically diversified because their ownership is divided among wallets.

Fractionalization divides the ticket.

It does not necessarily divide the risk.

## 2. MiamiCoin: useful to the city is not the same as owed to the holder

![Miami mayor Francis Suarez at the Bitcoin 2021 convention; historical context, not a photograph of the 2023 trading suspension.](article-assets/digital-cities/miamicoin-suarez.jpg)

*Miami mayor Francis Suarez at the Bitcoin 2021 convention; historical context, not a photograph of the 2023 trading suspension. Source: [Business Insider / Markets Insider](https://markets.businessinsider.com/news/currencies/miami-bitcoin-yield-miamicoin-crypto-francis-suarez-mayor-digital-wallet-2021-11).*

I do not want to flatten MiamiCoin into “another token that failed.”

The city received a tangible benefit.

In its **March 21, 2023** report, StateScoop noted that Miami had previously withdrawn **$5.25 million** from the project-linked wallet to fund rental assistance.

The same report covered OKcoin suspending MiamiCoin and NYCCoin trading because of limited liquidity. At that point, the suspension was described as temporary, and decentralized trading remained possible. [3]

For me, those facts expose the central distinction:

**A project can benefit a city without giving the token holder a protected economic interest in that benefit.**

Money reaching a housing program does not, by itself, give the buyer a claim on housing, redemption or municipal revenue.

I also keep the cities’ positions separate. In that report, New York’s mayoral office disclaimed affiliation with NYCCoin. Miami had taken control of its wallet and used funds. Those are not the same story. [3]

My takeaway is simple.

Before asking how much a token generated **for the city**, I ask what anyone owes **the holder**.

A city’s name on a coin is not the city’s signature on an obligation.

## 3. Dubai: a roughly $545 ticket, with institutions behind it

![Dubai skyline. Context photograph from coverage of the property-tokenization pilot; not an identified Prypco investment property.](article-assets/digital-cities/dubai-skyline.jpg)

*Dubai skyline. Context photograph from coverage of the property-tokenization pilot; not an identified Prypco investment property. Source: [Gadgets 360](https://www.gadgets360.com/cryptocurrency/news/dubai-real-estate-tokenisation-pilot-live-prypco-mint-8520060).*

On **May 25, 2025**, Dubai Land Department announced its property-tokenization pilot through Prypco Mint.

The minimum investment was approximately **$545**. At launch, participation was limited to UAE ID holders. Transactions used local fiat currency, not cryptocurrency. [4]

The small ticket is interesting.

The structure behind it is more interesting.

DLD’s announcement described tokenized shares in ready-to-own properties, property and risk disclosures, collaboration with VARA, the central bank and Dubai Future Foundation, and Zand as the pilot’s banking partner. [4]

That is a different order of construction:

Not “buy the token, and we will find the property.”

An identified property comes first. The investment mechanism is built around it.

I do not interpret regulatory involvement as a return guarantee. Vacancy, maintenance, valuation and exit risk remain. Nor do I treat the pilot’s original access rules as a description of eligibility today.

The announcement also projected tokenized assets reaching up to **7% of Dubai’s property market by 2033**, or roughly **$16 billion**.

That is a projection—not capital already deployed. [4]

My conclusion:

**Fractional access does not mean fractional legal work.**

Sometimes an investment app feels simple because a great deal of complicated work has been done behind it.

## 4. Hong Kong: scale the instrument, not just the announcement

![Victoria Harbour, Hong Kong. City context, not a photograph of the bond issuance.](article-assets/digital-cities/hong-kong-harbour.jpg)

*Victoria Harbour, Hong Kong. City context, not a photograph of the bond issuance. Source: [Wikimedia image via Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_Harbour).*

On **September 29, 2026**, Hong Kong’s government announced the pricing of its **fourth digital green-bond offering**, following three issuances since 2023.

The offering included a **$200 million US-dollar tranche**, with a three-year maturity and a **5.023%** rate.

For the Hong Kong-dollar tranche, tokenized deposits were incorporated into the primary-issuance settlement process. The announcement also identified the governing law, clearing system, platform, trustee and paying agent. [5]

That is the part I pay attention to.

Not just “a bond, but on-chain.”

An identifiable borrower. Defined payment terms. Named institutions. A settlement process. Successive iterations.

I cannot infer from that announcement that every earlier obligation has already matured and been repaid. Issuance history is not the same as a fully completed repayment record.

But I can see a useful development pattern: **repeat an identifiable instrument, improve the infrastructure, then broaden its use.**

That is a stronger model than treating a larger launch announcement as proof of progress.

## 5. Buenos Aires: I would rather have a useful assistant than an AI mayor

![Buenos Aires city-service chat in WhatsApp, pictured in historical coverage. This interface predates the newer agent architecture discussed below.](article-assets/digital-cities/boti-whatsapp.jpg)

*Buenos Aires city-service chat in WhatsApp, pictured in historical coverage. This interface predates the newer agent architecture discussed below. Source: [Clarín](https://www.clarin.com/ciudades/reconocido-whatsapp-chat-ciudad-atiende-110-mil-consultas-dia_0_ofSqIa-Hs.html).*

Urban-AI pitches often jump straight to autonomous government.

Boti gives me a more practical benchmark.

According to an AWS technical account co-written with representatives of the Buenos Aires government, the WhatsApp assistant launched in **February 2019**.

The authors describe more than **3 million conversations per month**, alongside a city website containing over **1,300 procedures** that the newer agent helps people navigate. [6]

I deliberately separate two things:

- Helping someone understand a procedure.
- Completing a legally effective service.

Explaining how to renew a driver’s license is not the same as issuing one.

The authors also report **up to 98.9% top-1 retrieval accuracy** in their evaluation. That measures a particular retrieval task. It does not mean every answer given to every resident is 98.9% correct. [6]

These qualifications are not distractions from the analysis.

They are the analysis.

I want to know what the number measures before deciding what it proves.

My takeaway:

**AI becomes useful to a city when it shortens the path to an outcome—not when it performs authority.**

A booking. An appointment. A relevant answer. A completed next step.

Less cinematic than an AI mayor.

Much easier to test.

## 6. Toyota: I cannot honestly say “never build a new city”

![Woven City in Susono, pictured in Reuters’ September 25, 2025 launch coverage.](article-assets/digital-cities/woven-city.jpg)

*Woven City in Susono, pictured in Reuters’ September 25, 2025 launch coverage. Source: [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/toyota-launches-woven-city-test-site-near-mt-fuji-tech-testing-2025-09-25/).*

A convenient conclusion would be that all purpose-built cities are mistakes.

My research does not justify it.

On **September 25, 2025**, Toyota announced the official launch of Woven City in Susono, following the concept’s unveiling at CES in **2020**.

The first employees and their families had begun moving in. Phase 1 was expected to accommodate about **300 residents**. Toyota also reported **20 Inventors** developing and testing products and services. [7]

What interests me is the bounded purpose: a real-world mobility test course.

Not proof that the perfect society has been solved.

I see an identifiable organizer, a specific experimental function and a limited initial scale.

I do not confuse occupancy with proven profitability. Launch demonstrates movement from concept to operation; it does not establish that the economics can be reproduced everywhere.

**Building something new is not the mistake. Replacing a testable purpose with an unlimited promise is.**

## The city I would actually build

These cases do not tell me to abandon ambition.

They tell me to separate promises.

I would start with an ordinary coastal city: restaurants, small hotels, workshops, events, residents and visitors who already return.

In fact, that is the direction I have started exploring with City Code.

Not another skyline.

A useful layer over a place that already has life.

**First: help someone act.**

Find the right place. Book a table. Get a ticket. Make an appointment.

Not another directory. A shorter route from question to outcome.

**Second: make returning worthwhile.**

A local partner network with tangible benefits and clear rules.

Who provides the benefit? Who funds it? What happens when a partner leaves or refuses to honor it?

**Third, separately: evaluate an investment instrument where it makes sense.**

If a business needs capital, I want an identifiable obligor, understandable cash flow, proper documentation and a default process.

The relevant legal structure depends on the jurisdiction. A membership key does not automatically become an investment right because both appear in the same app.

I refuse to blend three different promises:

“This will be more convenient.”

“This will save you money.”

“This may pay you a return.”

Each has its own economics.

Each needs its own accountability.

## The spreadsheet I want before the tokenomics

Here is a simplified example—not a live partner result or a return forecast.

A café records **100 purchases** attributed to a membership program, with a **$10 average ticket**.

The dashboard celebrates **$1,000 of sales**.

Assume a **40% contribution margin before discounts**. That leaves **$400**.

A **10% discount** across those purchases costs **$100**. Program servicing costs another **$50**.

Result: **$250 before fixed costs and taxes**—if every purchase is genuinely additional.

Now change one assumption.

Half those customers would have visited anyway.

Only **$500 of revenue** is incremental. Keeping the same discounts and servicing costs, incremental contribution falls to **$50**.

Same hundred purchases on the dashboard.

Five times less economic benefit.

Under those assumptions, the break-even incrementality rate is **37.5%**: the program needs enough genuinely additional purchases, contributing **$4 each**, to cover its **$150** cost.

This is why I ask not only how many people received a key, but how much additional money remained with the partner because of it.

And even that is not automatically investor income.

Remaining expenses, taxes, contracts and creditor priorities still come first.

**A smart contract can distribute money that reaches it. It cannot make an empty café profitable.**

## Seven rules I am taking forward

After these cases, I wrote down seven rules for my own work.

**1. Identify the right before choosing the token.**

Access, equity, debt and direct ownership are not synonyms.

**2. Check whether the asset actually reached the legal structure.**

A convincing interface is not evidence of a valid title or a funded reserve.

**3. Evaluate the operator, not just the code.**

Who maintains the property, pays the taxes and answers the claim?

**4. Separate public benefit from holder entitlement.**

A city receiving money does not tell me what a buyer can enforce.

**5. Measure incremental usefulness.**

Bookings, repeat visits and additional contribution matter more than keys issued.

**6. Never confuse transferability with liquidity.**

A transferable token still needs a willing buyer, an acceptable price and a legally workable transfer.

**7. Keep the first experiment narrow enough to evaluate.**

One service. One partner. One identifiable obligation. Then publish what happened—including what did not work.

None of these rules requires a blockchain.

A blockchain may help implement parts of the system.

It cannot replace the answers.

## The invisible city I can actually touch

I keep returning to Calvino.

Not because cities should remain stories, but because the stories make it so easy to stop looking.

I want to look at the ordinary things.

Did the booking work?

Did the partner honor the benefit?

Who repaired the heating?

Where is the money?

What happens when the answer is “no”?

That is how I now distinguish a digital city from a digital-city pitch.

**I would rather have a key to a working door than a token promising to build the city behind it.**

What city service would you use every week—even if it had no token at all?
